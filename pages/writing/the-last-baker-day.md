I turn the headlights off, it’s obvious they won’t be any use. Now that I’m here I can see there’s no point: the graves on this side near the gate are all old ones, covered with lichens, mustard-coloured spots and silver growing up from the lumpy ground. The dearly departed long forgotten, headstones listing. On the journey, the first streetlights had flicked on as I got to Yeovil. I was at a junction, caught behind a learner driver. I sat in the queue and watched a motorhome stutter and grind its gears as it took the road I was turning onto, and then a lumbering articulated lorry followed it, then a cement truck, too. An hour still to the churchyard before being caught behind those slow vehicles. I thumped the steering wheel. The lamp posts lit. I trundled along in the slow snake of vehicles and I got to wondering if the headlights might help when I got here, if I would be able to angle them at all. But it’s clear they won’t shine far enough.

There’s some light in the day still, the fading sky between the broken clouds turning only slowly from orange to purple, and I can see my way around the cemetery. I get my phone out anyway — I’ll need the flashlight for a torch to work out which is his, I’m guessing. Maybe it’ll be obvious, though, from the fresh-turned soil.

The gate is rusty and wedged on the gravel, but I lift a bit and it opens wide enough to pass through without needing a stronger shove. I wander between the gravestones, the shadowy bushes and ornamental trees. The churchyard turf is pitted and uneven and in the twilight I miss a ridge in the grass and half-roll my ankle.

The newest graves are on the far side, away from the road and near the bottom end of the cemetery. There’s four that are obviously recent, but only one has a wooden cross still. I switch on my phone’s torch. It’s just a simple plaque, and I can’t tell if it’s copper or something else in the blue-white light. I crouch. My ankle hurts. His name and dates are etched into it but that’s all. I’m none the wiser.

*Nicholas Maintry, 10 February 1973 – 30 August 2025.*

I count on my fingers. How can it be thirty-three years? I’m sorry, Nick.

There’s a rounded pebble in the turned earth, palm-sized. I didn’t think to bring anything with me, I don’t know why, and there’s nothing else here to mark his grave, so I grab the stone. The soil is heavy in clay and I clean most of it off with my fingers then wipe the rest on the dewey grass. Hardly a cricket ball shine, Nick, I know, but good enough. It’s pretty rubbish for a headstone, but I figure it’ll make him smile and I lean it against the wooden cross.

Saturday tomorrow. Don’t go anywhere — I’ll be back for a proper chat. Good to see you, Nick.

I return to the car, avoiding the risk of that ridge again. A crew of seagulls laughs at me above the churchyard. I hadn’t realised just how close this place is to the sea, just put the destination from the web page in the satnav, St Andrew’s Church, Weymouth, and drove. Although there’s a rise to the land and you can’t actually see any water from here, there’s nothing else beyond and I figure the Channel must actually be just over there. I stand on tiptoes, but I can’t see it.

I drive onto the bed and breakfast I have booked, and it occurs to me that maybe that’s why he’s chosen here: he’ll always be within reach of the sea.

---

In my room I unpack my bag, though it’s just an overnight stay so I’ve brought hardly anything really, clean underwear, a novel, toothbrush. The bay window has a view over the beach and the whole of Weymouth Bay, not that I can see much now it’s dark outside, and I realise I’m humming to myself as I stare. The promenade across the road is lit though, classic Victorian footpath dotted with classic Victorian seaside lamp posts, kept through the years for the benefit of the nostalgic. A row of white beach huts lines the shingle beyond. Vivaldi’s *Four Seasons*, the famous opening, that was the tune.

I’m hungry. I figure I’ll go for a walk along the prom, down the Esplanade. There’s bound to be something open.

My host, Mrs Pogonina, has brewed a fresh pot of tea when I come down from the room.

Sorry I was late—

I like to lock door at nine.

The remnant of a Russian accent. She’s already pouring; I can’t just walk out, it’d be impolite.

I should have called ahead, sorry, I say.

Here, some tea.

I take the cup, gratefully choose a shortbread from the biscuit plate she’s holding towards me, though it just accentuates the grumbling in my stomach.

Do you have a key I can use? I’m guessing there’ll be somewhere I can get a bag of chips.

Best in country, Feesh ’n‘ Fritz, on Market Street, she says, but I think it close at nine.

A local Weymouth law, is it? Everything closes at nine?

She stands without another word and leaves, and I hear shuffling and knocking in a drawer in the hallway.

Key. I go to bed at ten-thirty, after evening news.

It’s a lovely late September evening and I hum again as I walk beneath the Victorian lights, the same tune stuck in my head. I remember walking with Nick, listening to it. Nigel Kennedy’s version. Nick got it first. He was crazy about it and he told me, no, demanded that I get it too.

The Jubilee Clock on the Esplanade says it’s just gone nine thirty, but I don’t feel I can hurry. I’m not limping exactly, but my ankle is tender with a remnant of the stumble.

It came out in autumn 1989. That was the first year he was picked for the school rugby team and I wasn’t. 1989 was the beginning of things. 1989 we discovered grunge. 1989 was revolution. 1989 was Tiananmen Square, soon the Berlin Wall too. In autumn 1989 we were sixteen. I remember standing outside our house, Nick and me, loading the tape into our Walkmans, side 1, track 1. He’d give the countdown on his fingers for a synchronised start — 3, 2, 1, play! — and then we’d walk into town, in perfect unison. Down our hill to the Oxford Road and we’d hit all that crazy rapid bowing in the opening Allegro as we went past the Central Swimming Pool, doing air-violin like were at some industrial rock concert. It was a twenty-minute walk to Our Price Records on Broad Street, and we’d make it there by the end of Summer on the first side. Nigel Kennedy was our Kurt Cobain, and in the autumn of 1989, *The Four Seasons* was what *Nevermind* would be to us one day.

Fish ’n’ Fritz is still open, fortunately, and I join the line; I’ll let Mrs Pogonina know in the morning. I ask for a battered saveloy and regular chips. It’s scalding hot when I get it, so I leave the package wrapped and in the plastic bag, swinging the whole thing to cool it while I wander back towards the beach. The tide is high and I listen to the rhythmic wash of the waves as I eat. It beats Friday night chips on Broad Street. I can see why you liked it here, Nick.

I turn the tape over in my mind. Autumn, for the walk back to the guesthouse.

---

In bed, I scroll through the news headlines, then play a Sudoku. I know it’s not a good habit before sleeping, the blue light and hellish current affairs to think about, but they say routine is also important for a good night. It’s a tricky puzzle and takes longer than expected.

I’m thinking about that hold-up on the motorway that made everything so late. A crawl all the way past Bridgwater, and then when I got to the end of the queue it was something on the other carriageway and the tailback was six miles of rubbernecking. A tyre blown on a lorry and a white van that had run into the back end and spun out. The truck driver was stranded on the tarmac, on the phone for help. I guess I noticed him because of Nick. I wonder how long it took?

I went to university straight from college. Nick wasn’t the university type, and he got a job as a postman after school. We stayed in touch through my first year and whenever I went home we’d meet up for a pint. I remember laughing a lot, catching up on things, telling stupid tales for the crack. The stories were often about people the other didn’t know, places we’d been and things we’d done, but Nick had a way of telling tales with enough comedic panache and a healthy dusting of absurdity that he’d have me helpless in stitches, relentlessly adding details that lampooned whatever crazy thing some friend had done or, if necessary, how he’d embarrassed himself until he had me begging for mercy between gulps for air. I did the same too, tried my best at least, but he was funnier.

Nick always loved things with engines. Where I had maps from *National Geographic* magazine, his bedroom walls were covered with posters of Ferraris and Lamborghinis. He was the first of us to own a car. But it was Nick’s so it was not any old car: a Ford Escort XR3i, diamond white, wicked cloverleaf alloys, front spotlights, rear one-piece spoiler. It was the Millennium Falcon, just as worn out and every bit as fast a space cruiser, and to us it was the most magnificent machine ever to tear up the streets of our town.

That long university Easter holiday we cruised around in Nick Solo’s starship, talking about the world and who we were going to be in John Major’s Britain. We were interstellar smugglers, heroic adventurers in an uncharted galaxy, and the Force was with us.

Nick thought he’d be a driver, maybe try chauffeuring for celebrity A-listers. He could relocate to London, take pop stars from West End hotels to TV interviews in between recording studios or fancy restaurants, before going onto wild nightclubs, keeping secret whatever happened in the back seat. There was an opening, he reckoned, shuttling Hollywood-types from Heathrow to Elstree and back. Maybe he’d get Harrison Ford one day. He’d have to refuse if he was ever asked to pick up Sigourney Weaver, though: he’d just be staring in the mirror the whole time so he’d probably crash before even leaving the airport.

He took me back to university in that car. We motored up the M40 with a broken speedo, our necks bobbing as Stereo MC’s rattled the bodywork. He told me his plans had changed. Someone at the parcel depot had been talking about work on cruise ships, that there were openings for dealers in the casinos. He’d decided — he would do a course and become a croupier, then he’d travel the world.

Things drift apart easily, though, don’t they. Maybe it would’ve been different if I had known the man-hug in the carpark outside the university halls was the last time I’d see Nick Maintry. Maybe I would’ve said something different if I’d known there would be no more goodbyes.

---

I throw open the curtains to a bright morning: Weymouth faces the sun rising over the perfect blue sea. There’s windsurfers out already, I count five of them, and in the distance an early cross-Channel ferry heading out of port.

There’s only one other guest in the dining room. I say hello, and he nods but sticks to his phone screen. Not a talker.

There’s a coffee machine. I press a button. The man is still absorbed and his fork hovers over a wedge of black pudding.

Did you find something? Mrs Pogonina has a rack of toast and she chooses a table for me.

I’m sorry?

Last night?

Oh, thanks, yeah. That place you said was still open. Good suggestion. Different rules for Fridays, or something.

I’m not so much of a breakfast person these days but my host does not approve of just coffee it would seem, so I ask for a poached egg.

Is there, like, a scuba school or something round here? I ask when she returns.

It’ll be all booked today, the other guest says. He looks like a walker. Maybe a birder.

No, no. I don’t want to dive.

Oh?

Just wondering. For a friend.

No need to say more. No need to say that my friend is dead. No need to say I don’t know why. No need to say that my heart stopped beating when I discovered. No need to say that I don’t know if it has started again.

Still, he nods.

You’ll find at the town harbour, I believe, says Mrs Pogonina.

If it’s wrecks you want, though, the man says, down Portland way’s the best. You see ’em in the water there, regular like.

It was something my mother had said. She had been to two funerals in quick succession, people she thought she knew but then in the eulogies, and in the conversations afterwards, realised there were huge swathes of time and events that she had never heard about. She found that she knew only a scrap of them, she said. And she wished she’d had more time: time for the conversations, about normal stuff and deep things, too, time for the those things you only realise are important when it’s too late.

It made me think of you, Nick. Not straight away. But a couple of days later, when I was doing something, I don’t know, the washing up or bringing in the supermarket shopping or something else mundane, I thought of you. I stood there in the kitchen with my phone in my hand. You have one of those names, Maintry — uncommon, not many search results. I looked and I saw. The first thing: a memorial notice, from just a month ago. My heart stopped. Your name, right there. I clicked. A location, a date and time, and a button to donate to your charity of choice, to do with blood donors. That was all. I’m still none the wiser.

The sun is still climbing outside, barely a cloud. More windsurfers now, and some kite-boarders, too. Looks fun, though it’s not for me — fun to look at, I guess, not something to start in my fifties. I remember seeing an M&S in the town last night where I’ll be able to buy some flowers or something, so I throw my bag in the car and walk the same route. The ankle seems ok. The promenade looks different in daylight, easier to imagine in its Victorian heyday. I pass a few dog-walkers, retired couples walking hand-in-hand, a shopper or two pulling a canvas trolley. By the helter-skelter there’s three youngsters pulling tricks on skateboards while an old boy watches. From a distance I guessed he was about to tell them off, but up closer I can see he’s happy watching, enjoying the effort they put into the tricks until they get them.

It’s a pretty place, Weymouth, its former self reimagined for modern times. Now I’m here I can see why you ended up settling, Nick.

Roses seem appropriate, when I get to the shop. I pick up some sandwiches too, posh ones from here, and a drink for lunch later.

---

I can’t remember now how I first met Nick, no idea when or where it was we first knew of each other. He used to say it was in Physics class, in our second year, when he got separated from Dan Stockton for talking and made to sit beside to me on the front row. Over the years I’ve just let that become the truth. Whatever it was, we connected quickly: I remember sharing a table in French and History. And we both tried out for the rugby team, like I said. He was taller than me and was put in the second row with the school’s secret weapon, Dan Stockton. I played number eight; I was strong and good in the scrum, but really just not fast enough on my feet. Mr Guittings taught us Physics. Nick was convinced he was victim of a vendetta. Guittings isn’t there in my memories of later years, so he must’ve retired that summer or the next. I guess I owe him a debt of thanks.

We lived close to each other, just a few streets between us in the older parts of Reading closer to the town centre. I guess that’s one reason why we connected. Most of the others in the year lived the other side of school, in the newer suburbs. We had that walk home, just the two of us, through the park and then the alleyways and back streets. BMX had just become a thing and we’d mime our moves across the grass, taking huge jumps off the wooden edging, bar spin in the air, or superman (Nick loved a superman), nail the landing, end in a dramatic tail skid and rapturous applause from the crowd. Perfect moves are easy when you practice without a bike. There was the house with the stupid aggressive dog — a tiny thing, a Scottie or Jack Russell or something, but the loudest dog in town — and Nick would terrorise it when we went past. Then one day the gate was open and I didn’t realise, and the dog raced silently up the side-alley and suddenly yapped at my ankles and I almost soiled myself. It went for me and only got my trouser leg but it managed to gouge a hole in them, vicious thing. Nick laughed the whole way home. I was always wary after that, passing that side-alley.

There was another reason for our friendship: we were boys without fathers. We never really talked about it, but it was the truth. I guess we recognised it in each other.

Nick’s parents had separated. I don’t remember when it happened; I didn’t ask. He lived with his mother and his sister in an upstairs flat above his grandmother. Nice house, a bit squashed when you’re home is only half. She was Church of Scotland, his grandmother, and strident with it, demanding he go with her to her own St Andrew’s for the big ones, Easter, Christmas, harvest, saint’s day in November. I guess that’s another reason for you to get buried in that particular cemetery. He saw his father occasionally, a weekend here or there, came back with some money and a promise of some future trip to Alton Towers or Concorde or Florida or something, things that never materialised. Nick’s dad bought him his first ever scuba lesson, though, his sixteenth birthday. A gift first time like a drug dealer, and he got him hooked.

My father? Well, he was sick, long-term in hospital. And then Easter ’86, he died. Nick understood. Without words, he knew what it felt like to be left behind, to be left alone, at least in part. A boy without a dad, with all that growing up to do. There were two of us, though, in it together.

I remember the school closures, back in those days. I think the teacher strikes started some time in 1985, when we were third years. For a while, the school would close at lunchtimes, all pupils off the grounds for the hour. I took a packed lunch but Nick made us go to the chippy, and then we had this log in the woods in the park. We’d talk about the girls who never saw us let alone consider dating us, such magnificent and mystical beings who had condescended to step out of paradise for long enough to study Shakespeare and oxbow lakes and Agincourt before departing into the heavenly realm once more come the bell at twenty-past-three. One lunchtime it rained heavily and we agreed there was time to walk home rather than stand around and get drenched. He started it: stomped in a puddle and soaked my trousers. The next, I got the splash high enough almost to hit his face. We didn’t make it back that afternoon.

Later, they became full-day stoppages. At the time, none of us kids really understood the strikes. What teachers did generally was confusing, but this had us completely perplexed. Why were they so angry at a day off? Were they so fed up they couldn’t face teaching us anymore?

The strikes ended in 1987, but there were still days off — they called them ‘Baker Days’ after Kenneth Baker, the Education Secretary, part of the settlement between the unions and the government. All the pupils out so the staff could have training. Nick and I, we’d take our BMXs to the pump track by the river. He was fearless and it was his greatest strength and his undoing. Once, he missed the take-off point on the double rollers and came down with all his speed on the second as it rose up to him. He hit it hard and knocked himself out. I sprinted over to him but I was 14 — how could I know what to do? When he came round, his bike frame was snapped clean in two. We never told the whole truth.

---

There is nothing obvious in Weymouth harbour, and I figure he would have been into diving for shipwrecks so I go back to the car. I get a few smirks and an approving nod or two as I carry the flowers back up the promenade. There’s no need to wield a pin and burst their presumptions.

The satnav says there’s a scuba place on the south side of Portland harbour, about a twenty-five minute drive. It takes me on a route down residential roads to avoid some sort of traffic jam. Seaside towns have a particular look, Victorian grandeur that slides into interwar pebbledash. I come out onto the main road behind a tipper truck, a big beefy thing that dwarfs my car. Nigel Kennedy plays the Four Seasons again as I drive, ‘Summer’ now, languid and sweltering and then the sudden rhythmic intensity of relentless heat. It’s becoming warm today, an Indian Summer for the end of September.

When I went home that summer at the end of my first year, Nick had already gone. I pictured him in a pristine white uniform and, for some reason, a flat cap, on the sundeck of a huge ship in the blue Caribbean Sea, a boat filled with wealthy retirees from Texas and Florida and Sweden and Australia. I scraped by on my student grant, and I had images of him flirting with the cougar guests, helping them feel young again, then later sipping cocktails with the spectacular hostesses. For the next year or two we left messages for each other with our mothers, when we would be home or not, when we might be able to meet up. There were no mobile phones; ship-to-shore was beyond our means. We never managed to synchronise, and before long the notes dried up.

The last time I heard anything he had stopped by my mum’s house and stayed for a cup of tea. He’d had enough of the cruise ships, she relayed later. It’d been fun while it lasted, but the reality of it was a far cry from the romantic image he had imagined. There’s only so many perfect sunsets you need to see before they become run-of-the-mill. It had got to him never having anything other than a cabin to call home. He was going to retrain as a driver, he had told her, like he had once thought, except for HGVs. A guaranteed job, he had said, the world would always need stuff delivered.

The doors are open at the dive school, but I don’t see anyone around. Across the carpark I spot a handrail for a gangplank, so I wander over to the water’s edge. There’s a yellow boat tied up to a pontoon below, and there’s a man onboard working on something or other.

Can I help you? He raises a hand to shield his eyes from the glare.

I was just —

Everything ok? Are you lost or something?

No, no. It’s just … well, just a long shot.

He puts down whatever it is he’s been fixing, walks up the ramp.

I can’t take you out, sorry.

Oh, no, that’s ok.

There’s a dive out already. Went at, like, 8:30 this morning. Only one this weekend, I’m afraid.

Yeah, I’m not a diver.

Oh, so is it —

My friend is.

Oh right. Are you after a gift card, or something?

Or was.

Was?

He lived in Weymouth, was into scuba. Used to be, anyway. I came by to look him up.

What’s his name? I know most of the locals. Small world, y’know.

Uh, it’s, ah, Nick, Nick Maintry.

Ah.

Do you know him? I say.

Yeah, I know him. Knew him. The man has hold of the back of his neck. Were you friends? he says.

We were at school together. We lost touch.

Right. I presume you know —

Yeah. Online.

---

The coffee is from a machine, but it fits the bill. Luke, the diver guy, drags a table and a couple of chairs into the sun outside the building.

I didn’t know Nick well, he says, by reputation mostly. I’ve been out a few times with him, but he went with the crew round by the marina usually. He points past the building. He liked to do Boadicea — navy ship, sunk in the Channel at the end of the War. It’s a bit deep for us, fifty-plus metres. We do mostly tourist stuff here, thirty max.

I wasn’t sure if he’d kept it up.

He was mad for it.

Just thought I’d come by and ask. To see, you know.

He was fearless, Nick was.

Yeah.

A good buddy down there, nice and calm, patient. Proper experienced, really good to dive with if you wanted to push yourself.

I haven’t seen him in years.

The sun dips behind a cloud for a minute. I set my paper cup down, half-full.

Do you know how he, you know —

Oh, has no one said —

I’ve only seen what’s online. Not much there, beyond the dates, the churchyard.

Right. I don’t know that much, to be honest. They didn’t say much at the funeral.

I only found out by chance, really.

You don’t do you, Luke says. Funerals. You talk about how they lived, not how they died.

Like I said, we kinda lost touch, so —

I know it was an accident.

An accident?

You know he was a driver, yeah?

I’d heard, yep.

A crash, on the motorway.

No.

Didn’t stand a chance, is what I heard.

Jeez.

A minute’s silence is all that’s possible. I feel the ache in my ankle. A microlight drones against the sky overhead.

I thank Luke, the diver guy, and he takes my empty paper cup. I want to walk, but there isn’t really anywhere around here, all built up for the port. I know the other side of the island is the end of Chesil Beach, just a couple of minutes in the car. On the shore, I reach for a few pebbles from the shingle, not quite palm-sized, and hurl them one by one into the sea, waiting for each plop. I sit on the gravel berm to eat my posh sandwiches and stare at the vast blue expanse. It’s not a single thing, not a uniform sea colour, and changes with all subtlety from shore to steely horizon. I wonder where out there Nick’s navy ship, Boadicea, is wrecked, fifty metres down.

When I’ve finished I fold the wrapper into my pocket then stumble back down the shingle to the waterline. There’s been no wind the last day or two and the wash is gentle, and the stones chatter in short sentences with the crashing waves. Every seventh wave is bigger, they say, and it catches me surprise. I jump in it, for Nick and for old time’s sake, a last Baker Day.

---

The graveyard looks different today. It has that particular English beauty in the sunshine, ash trees, yew, ornamental cherry, and the ancient headstones that could have been extruded from the Dorset bedrock every bit as much as set in the earth intentionally. I find I am wandering, drawn to meandering without deciding at all. The roses crinkle in the cellophane, and I carry a pebble I’ve brought with me from the shoreline. It’s probably not really allowed, taking a stone from Chesil Beach, but I’ve done it anyway. In my palm it is no longer cold, taking heat and memory through my skin.

There are stories in these stones. In places, the same family name repeated, all buried together over years and generations. Couples, lovers, or siblings maybe, dead within months of each other. It’s hard to see the date on the oldest graves, but I find one for someone who died in 1827. Almost 200 years ago. Before then, did we not want to remember? Did we not care?

I’ve made my way to the bottom end without realising it, and there is my old friend’s grave.

I have another stone for you, Nick, I say. I don’t know why I’m speaking out loud, it’s not something I do normally, it just happens and it feels like the right thing. I thought you’d like to have something from the sea, I say, from near your ship I think. Well, from the beach.

I hadn’t spotted him when I first arrived but I see now there’s someone else here, a man. There were a few cars parked in the lane and I assumed they were just the residents of the houses that overlook the dry stone wall of the churchyard. He looks in my direction but he’s not coming this way, and I’m glad of that.

My knees click as I crouch, and I groan, then laugh at myself. I place the Chesil Beach pebble next to the other, the beginnings of a cairn. The cellophane is noisy as I unwrap the flowers and I’m struck by how unusual it is, to give red roses to another man. We haven’t spoken for thirty years, Nick, we have this huge unexplored cavern in each other’s lives and yet, yes, it is a kind of love that I have for you, my friend. I place the flowers on the bare earth. I fold the cellophane, once, twice, again, and pocket it. I re-place the roses for a better lay.

Did you know him?

It’s the man. I didn’t hear his approach. He has flowers also, in oranges and whites, though I couldn’t tell you the varieties. Chrysanthemum comes to mind, but I don’t know if that’s right.

Sort of, I say, still crouching. We were at school together.

I stand.

You’re a friend, are you? I ask.

Yes. Well, work colleague, really. But a friend too.

He holds out a hand. Pierre.

More than a friend. Nick was a … well, he has magic blood.

---

I bring the tea to the table on a plastic tray, a pot and two cups, a jug of milk. I’ve chanced a slice of flapjack each, too, presuming this man, Pierre, will be happy with something sweet.

You said you work together? I say, then correct myself, worked together?

I’m a depot manager at Bridgwater. Nick’s a driver, does C1s — artics, that is.

I share out the cups, the cakes, put the tray aside.

Doing what he wanted to do, when we were growing up.

A little boy’s dream, eh, big machines.

Yeah. Though he wanted to chauffeur movie stars.

Of course he did.

Pierre is a nice guy, but an over-explainer. He tells me things about working together, the convoluted happenings at a distribution centre, the pressures, the politics, the laughs. A story for everything, and a digression in every story. I like him. And the Nick that is a character in all of Pierre’s tales is the same Nick who would splash in the rain without fearing consequences, who would risk breaking his bike and himself to achieve immortality, who would sail the world for the romance of it, who would give himself to help you, to pass on the important stuff on an easy dive so that maybe one day you can join him on a greater adventure, who would take you home even when he knew it was taking you apart.

And I’m jealous of this man who knows the Nick Maintry that I don’t know at all, the version of my missing friend who can’t be summed up in a few minutes with a cake and a cup of tea. I listen to Pierre and I laugh at his stories, the things he’s done and places he’s been with Nick at work and outside, and I wonder if I would have done them too, if I would have been there too, if we had not let things drift.
