<span class="text-uppercase bold">The table is still set,</span> if you can call it that at the end of the meal, the detritus scattered across it, and Hannah feels the urge to get a cloth to wipe away the spilt water, the stray shavings of parmesan, the oily drips of salad dressing, but she doesn’t move. Not yet. She is unable to move, in truth, because she is waiting and while she waits she stares.

She is dressed for the occasion, dressed to receive as her mother would have said. Though it was just the two of them, her and Leo, had only ever been the two of them, no friends or guests invited, and only a minor celebration, to herald the weekend. Though it was the end of a climactic week for her, an important week, a watershed. And he had to leave to meet friends across the city for their wild weekend in Wales, downhill bikes in the Black Mountains, so it had its natural morphology anyway, this meal. They both knew that.

She has sat here for an hour now, staring. The kitchen window this side looks toward the road, the low ledge affording a grandstand view of traffic and passers-by though not of callers to her door. Her gaze has barely altered from the street’s strange serenity all this time, waiting for the cab, waiting for the doorbell, waiting for the jangle of keys and the heavy clunk of the twisted lock. Still she waits and it feels like anticipation in her mind, of runners in some kind of marathon, out of sight but coming, though maybe this one is just a two-horse race between Leo and Guy Verhoeven. Yes, he had left, Leo, and she had watched him cross the road and put a hand out to ``feel for the flint wall as he turned the corner and she sent Guy a message. And also she thought, maybe he will come back.

Little left of the arrabbiata on their plates, a measure of a good meal. Individual side-salads, in those rustic bowls he likes, from the holiday last summer, the village pottery in Mallorca. His is empty but she has a few leaves left, and she takes a piece of Lollo Rossa and wipes it through the dressing on the rim of his, wondering what it tastes like, whether it’s even noticeable (she hadn’t dared before). He always liked a good salad. Liked the variety of things in one bowl. Liked trying new things, new elements, new variants. So that worked. But maybe he had noticed.

---

When the company is 50 or 60 people you see all the new hires, you even interview most of them, but Secronia went past 500 a year back and its rapidly closing on 650 (although the projections had them pushing the thousand mark by now, so lagging behind), and of course there’s no way to know everyone. People come, people go. Some faces you see more often, some just stand out for whatever reason. That was Leo Abbott. His height, of course — he stood out literally. But she had been seeing him lots, or noticing him, maybe that was a better way to put it. Just coming out of the lift or up the rear stairs from a lunch break, or walking past the window towards the kitchen coffee machine, or chatting with colleagues outside the meeting rooms. He had a way, an ease that she noticed, a comfort in himself, in his skin or whatever. And when he smiled at her, way, way back that first time she saw him, actually saw him, she felt the warmth of the sun on her face. Maybe because she rarely felt it. Not that it was rare for people to smile at her, but she had a compass for it with opposite polarity that meant she was repelled by sycophancy or fawning or anything resembling. So, his smile: yes, his smile. It was simple, somehow genuine if that makes sense, generous without need of anything, which was what Hannah had noticed and kept on noticing over the weeks.

Now she realises it was engineered, probably. Or so she suspects and has done for a while. Designed, at least.

But not at the time.

Which is strange, because he was forward. It was Leo who had asked her out for dinner, for instance. Magnetic repulsion, instantly, no way that she would even consider such a thing, let alone as a co-founder, except not with him because there was something in the way he said it that meant she had agreed without realising she had done so and they were sat opposite at a rickety table in this little Italian place that’s just opened up, back of nowhere, a passion project of some friends of his, authentic Milanese: he swept you up, and there they were, with winding but hilarious stories, and better questions, and listening, and following, and laughing, and self-deprecating. And there they were in Tate Modern at a Frida Kahlo exhibition, and there they were standing in line for amazing Mexican street food afterwards. And there they were in Aldeburgh at some ungodly hour for the rising of the solstice sun, and tasting English sparkling wine on the return through the Essex sticks, and there she was dozing in the passenger seat. And there they were in Milan with his friends’ friends, and there they were gazing at The Last Supper with their own eyes. And there he was, presenting to the board. And there he was with roses and a getaway in a treehouse for their three-month-iversary. And there she was, falling blindly. And there they were in a vintage furniture store, choosing two perfect armchairs in teal velvet for the sunny window for reading and listening. And there they were with the papers on a Sunday morning. And there he was listening to Guy’s thoughts on the ideal wine pairing (were one able to get hold of the stuff) and questioning her Secronia partner’s judgement and thereby his palate, which was novel. And there he was brushing his teeth, and there he was hanging his shirts in the wardrobe, and there he was with a cool cloth for her forehead to bring the flu temperature down and squeezing navel oranges for the freshest juice to fight off the symptoms. And there they were.

And there they were.

---

It’s the things on the kitchen work surface that she needs to clear. She knows it but doing so, actually standing to do it, requires that she detach from her past self and face the other direction.

She can be a messy chef, she knows that. She focuses on the end-point; the getting there is just the series of things to be done, and spillages happen so someone should be there to clean up. This evening, though, that’s her. And the longer she leaves it the more urgent it becomes.
That’s why she had started this venture with Guy. Secronia. Yes, its start was fairly informal, a pie and a drink and a chat in The Star & Anchor, but it was more of a pitch than anything else. Guy was a mark, or so he was to her. He did the late morning keynote at the Helix conference in 2012, and they happened to be standing together in the lunch queue after. Conversation was easy: his three years on the Wellcome Genome Campus taking a life sciences start-up to something like maturity, her frustrated post-doctoral career and the inherent stagnation in big pharma, mutuals, histories, ambitions, the usual, and he was easy to talk with, funny too. But he was called away to some panel. She cyberstalked him for a while but got bored eventually, or just forgot. Then there he was in that 2014 press event with the Prime Minister and Business Sec. that made the rounds right about the time the thing that would become Secronia was building in her mind and into something demo-able on her laptop. She knew it had to be him. A few manufactured overlaps, oh, it’s you, just by chance. An email or two. A phone call or two. And then the pie and the pint was the easy part. Guy was the man, her wielder of the ax, the one to bring order and precision to her vision. Who would’ve thought Guy Verhoeven loved a British pub pie?

A figure passes on the road outside, but she had been playing with the stem of the wineglass, only sees the motion in the side of her eye and only catches a foot, a man’s foot, when she looks up. Guy, or Leo, the two horses. Or just someone else.

She stands, an involuntary movement but is now standing. She takes the glasses to the sink, rinses the dregs. She will wash them in a moment, but first things first.

She, that messy chef, chastises herself. There are trimmings all over the countertop, lettuces and cabbages and chicory and camouflage for other kinds of leaves, and tomato stalks and spilt juice, pepper seeds, shards of red onion and radish, green bean ends, some fragments of feta, leaves and trimmings of leaves of all types. She sweeps everything onto the chopping board, carefully and precisely, then into a kitchen waste bag, carefully and precisely. She takes a spray gun to the surface, wipes it all with kitchen roll. She lifts anything that may be hiding a remnant. The spent paper goes also into the waste bag and she ties the handles and takes it out immediately to the compost bin in the garden. Out of sight. In the kitchen again: she washes her hands thoroughly, to make sure they are completely clean of any residue or contamination. A bowl of hot, soapy water — for the glasses, the crockery, anything else that should be guiltless.

There, all good.

She fills the kettle and decides to use the toilet while it boils. On the cold seat, the only sound in the whole house is the flow onto the porcelain. The door is ajar on the cupboard under the sink, and she opens it. It’s still there. A stick, they call it, though it’s made of plastic. And the line is still there, in the window. She pushes the door so it clicks closed on the catch.

In the kitchen again, she makes a hot drink: some peppermint tea, as a digestif. No alcohol now, so they say, though there was the wine of course.

Her phone is ringing.

---

‘So why was it you went with Guy?’ Leo says.

It’s an innocent evening not long ago, a few post-work drinks, a meal, then walking the South Bank in the pinkness towards a tube, but she feels the sharp pinch at their naivety.

‘I’ve told you all that already.’ They drop hands to dodge a heedless night runner. ‘Hundreds of times. Haven’t I?’

‘Yeah, but, like, you didn’t know him already, did you. Like, from university or whatever, so I was wondering, why him. Why not some Harvard MBA or whatever?’

It’s the first time she’s had that in the four years, they’ve been together, no sting or tingle ever, and it surprises her: first, that she felt it as a pricking of her thumbs, and second, that it’s only happened now. It dissipates in moments, a couple of heart beats is all it takes to forget the nick and she tells him afresh about that first meeting with Guy, about the gush of setting out the idea, painting the picture with broad brush strokes and fine detail, of that weird thing he does of enthusiasm-but-caution, the thing she appreciates the most, that it took eight months of work, the first two to bring him round, the next six of his laborious due diligence. And he grins as she talks, which is weird because business talk kills romance for her but he says he loves how she bubbles and fizzes as she speaks of it all. But there is a residue, she finds, that smarting in her thumbs, there even as they swipe through the barriers and take the escalator down to the platform. The train to Balham is noisy and talking is hard, but thinking is easy. And it’s worse later in the dead of night and the dread of quiet minutes between things in the diary and it prickles and she rubs at it in her mind until the sore begins to itch. Was she, the very model of a modern major geneticist, vulnerable?

Those things nagging at her thoughts? That system upgrade that dropped a whole drive two years ago. Thank Heaven for the backups — stale, but they had recovered two-thirds of what had gone. But there was experimental data loss the following quarter. Then, missed targets, plural. The knock-on impact on funding, on the client base, on progress generally. Leo was nowhere near most of it, nothing in his departments at all. Maybe she was just making beasts from shadows. And yet he always seemed to be on hand, to help with the recovery or whatever. Just eager kindness? Or something else?

The Max Planck trip was the time to tell Guy, she had decided.

‘Coincidence,’ he said. ‘Two and two do not add up to cucumbers.’ For a second, he is bemused as much as her. ‘Or a gunpowder plot. He’s in love with you, he just wants to do what he can.’

She lets it go.

Until she can’t any longer.

---

Her phone is on the table, ringing. Don’t run, silly goose, you’ll trip and hurt yourself. You need to think of these things now.

‘It’s me,’ a male voice.

Though it is not him. It is Guy Verhoeven.

‘Are you home?’ he asks, in that confident manner of the executive class, the highly educated. ‘Are you alone?’

‘Where are you?’

‘I’ll be there in thirty. Less if I can.’

‘Any word?’

‘I’ll tell you when I get there. Don’t do anything rash.’

She considers the room. It is well cleaned, and she nods. Yet, there’s a nagging in her mind and she’s not sure why. The rhubarb is cooled after stewing earlier, now in a container in the fridge, for breakfast, or whatever.

She considers the house. Maybe there’s something somewhere that’s needling. She puts her keys away in the drawer, checks the post. She straightens the magazines on the table in the lounge, puts the TV remote on the side under the screen. The hall floor is fine, no mop needed; the maid service came this morning. And the bedsheets are all clean today, obviously. None of that.

She considers the garden. She didn’t think to check the beds when she went out earlier. Maybe something tore, fragments of leaves or something, though that’s just normal stuff, nothing given away there. It’s dark outside.

She considers herself. The past is unclear and the recent and the distant are smeared into each other. Leo is not here, she knows that at least, but now she forgets quite why. She had it before but it’s gone blurry. Maybe he didn’t take the news too well. She thought he would, she wanted him to. He’ll be a good father, she knows that. The house is quiet.

---

It strikes her like a clumsy fall, when she realises, when she actually understands. She is walking around the lake in lieu of a lunch break with the half-eaten remains of something already she can’t remember in a paper bag and she comes to a halt, winded, unable to breathe, as if she has tripped, landed on her back and knocked the air clean out of her lungs.

It is the evasion. Things just stacking up on themselves.

The things at Secronia, yes, but there were good reasons for all those and as Leo said, as they all said, as you do when you’re working so hard to build this thing — everything was a joint effort. You win as a team, and you fail as a team. There was no point pinning anything on one person in particular, because there never was any one person in particular, no single action or inaction that was the sole determinant of any given outcome. Things happen. Disappointments. Triumphs. Everything, always, as one team. They all stood by that. Absolutely.

But it’s other stuff, too.

He never talks about his mother, for instance. His father was military, killed in Desert Storm in ’91, he had said, so-called friendly fire. He never knew him, nothing to say about him other than that. Neither brothers nor sisters, no siblings of any stripe. If she asks about his mother he changes the subject, which for the longest time has frustrated her. It is so confusing. She had presumed he would be as close to his mother as she is to hers. They talk on the phone twice a week, often more. Her mother is her first choice for a travel companion. It took her a while to realise what he was doing; it was a gift, a talent of his, as she has come to see it, the subtlety. Sometimes humour, sometimes misdirection, other times simply talking past the question and pulling her with him in the flow of things, and the romantic glow did the rest. She has considered all manner of reasons why the woman is missing. The ordinary: alienated by a devastating argument. The sensational: packed away in an institution of one kind or another. The exotic: a mysterious holiday liaison and swept off into a globetrotting life of wild luxury. What lubricant there may have been in the halo of passion, of lust, of love, ran dry after a while, one year or two, and Hannah began to feel the heat of friction, to perceive a curtain parting to reveal Oz stood behind, just a man. More. The friends he has, as close and countless as they may be, are from the now time. People from the cycle club, the ones he is going to see. People from the gym, from work. Graeme is the only auld acquaintance, and they meet for beer and banter the second Thursday of each month. She has met Graeme twice: in the dark for Black Panther at the cinema, and months later, dropping off a battered novel he had borrowed. All else of Leo’s life is hazy, his history elusive, and she feels, has felt increasingly, that his story is perforated, porous even. Now she castigates herself for the missed signs, for overlooking them or looking around them, finds it hard to comprehend there was a time she, the empirical observer, the woman of hard science, was also the naïf.

She waits for Guy and she wants to call her mother, flicks the handset awake. She feels the urge of the penitent, though she is not Catholic. A thumb hovers, twitches, stops. She puts the phone down, face to the table, where it levitates on a ring of light that suddenly fails and lets it fall to earth.

---

There’s a file on her work cloud drive, encrypted and buried deep in the architecture. To the casual observer it looks like a run-of-the-mill system file. It’s a hybrid thing, both journal and catalogue; she’s not proud of that. She has it open only when she needs to, to record something — an event, a thought, a memory, a question — and scrupulously removes its access state from the application history when she’s done.

Created: September 2022. Because she was doubting her sense of reality. Everyone went a bit weird in the pandemic; some, a lot. Strange corners of the internet, theories for this, conspiracies in that. Separated, distanced, alienated. Most were trying to get back to normal, so called, some time in late ’22, early ’23. Hannah read the science and felt sympathy for Cnut, his empty resistance to the incoming tide. A geneticist understands the hazards, the consequences, the vital need for precautions, the right time for decompression.

Doubt has never been her way. Her mind, her memory and recall, her reasoning: these are all elements of her nature that, she feels, has always felt, define her, the core constituents of her nature, her fundamental particles. One of her greatest fears of infection, of the pandemic’s long separation, of the quarantimes, was the loss of herself. Brain fog was a shortcut term, but it was those essential qualities she feared losing: slowed thinking, memory failures, blunted focus. Who was she without her mind? Hannah caught the virus three times; she never felt her power diminish, yet he would say, ‘you’re so forgetful,’ most days.

On the doorstep:

‘Dammit, have you got your keys? Mine are in the desk at work.’

‘Always on me. You’re so forgetful.’

In the supermarket:

‘Have we got everything for the tagine tonight?’

‘Cinnamon sticks. And prunes. You’re so forgetful.’

More often, though, him:

‘Last thing for the slide deck — who were the partners in the 2019 raise?’

‘What? That was ages ago.’

‘Oh, come on, really. Two years.’

‘Uh, excuse me. A little something’s happened in between. You may not’ve noticed.’

‘You’re so forgetful. Pass me your laptop, I’ll look it up.’

So she has kept a list, encrypted, buried. And in it, slowly, grew a pattern, and with it she found another word, gaslighting, a shortcut for something far more complex. For a while she considered ‘coercive control’, but it’s not that. She is not isolated. If anything, the opposite: what she has noted and annotated over time points instead at her connectedness, that his interest, she can see, is directed — at her role and her influence and who she knows and who knows her. It’s subtle because that is his gift, that is his talent, but it’s there, in the data. And when you plot all the things on some sort of network graph (because Hannah has done that, also buried, also encrypted), the questions and the forgetting and the probing and the absences and the meet-ups, and  you overlay it on the technical issues and corrupted data and business targets narrowly missed and presentations that fail to land and reluctant profit warnings, then there’s another pattern to see. Guy gets it.

Updated: this morning.

---

Leo, come home. I’m sorry. I’m sorry for throwing my wine at you and walking out of the restaurant on Valentine’s Day. I’m sorry for shaming you in front of the board, for yelling with the full range of what you call my founder’s voice, for demeaning your work in public. I’m sorry for speaking to Guy in a hushed voice behind a hand and asking him to find someone with special skills and discover everything he can. And I’m sorry, I’m so completely sorry, I’m sorry for being rash, for feeding you what I knew you should not eat. So Leo, come home. Please, just be here now, and I will care for you and I will minister to you, and we will understand, and we will make it all better.

---

He had left, Leo, and she had watched him cross the road and put a hand out to feel for the flint wall as he turned the corner and she sent Guy a message, and now she lifts her head to the noise of a car engine, arriving on this sultry early summer evening like guilt. This vehicle slows but passes by and its specific voice is soon forgotten.

Her peppermint tea is ended. She squeezes the bag on the inside of the mug, then crosses the room to drop it into the pristine compost bin. She swirls the cup and for a second considers swallowing the last bitter end, then tosses that also, down the sink. She waits and she wants a drink, a strong one. No alcohol now, they say, but her imagination is pouring already, and she is sipping it until he arrives, the winner of the two-horse race.

She turns to another reproachful engine that calms to a growl at the kerb outside. She is already standing. One glance at the surfaces, the sideboard, the sink, the floor.

A car door slams.

Footsteps.

Doorbell. Not the jangle of keys.

It is Guy Verhoeven who stands before her now, in the kitchen, sealed in amber. He has a transparent blue plastic wallet tucked under his arm, padded with paper. In this hyper-digital age, printed matter has its unique security.

He looks only at her, does not survey the room, not scanning for traces of betrayal, of rashness, of mystic belladonna or phantom laburnum seeds or actual rhubarb leaves.

‘You’re shaking,’ he says.

She turns away, toward the back of the house where two perfect armchairs in teal velvet rest in the window soaked in moonlight.

‘Drink?’ he says.

She shakes her head.

‘Well, I will.’ He pours a finger’s worth of the best she has, takes it straight, no nonsense.

---

Guy pops open the wallet, pulls out a few sheets in full performance. Some are stapled, some not; he shuffles through them to check the order. He looks directly at her.

‘He’s not who he says he is, you were right,’ he says. ‘Our Leo is not Leander Abbott. His real name is Louis. Louis Prior.’

He passes her a single piece of paper, a photograph printed at the top. He is smiling, of course. That was always part of her attraction to him, how easily a smile comes, or maybe that for him it’s just natural because there’s amusement and absurdity to be found in everything.

‘Louis,’ she says. She touches his face and she wonders with Juliet, what’s in a name?

Guy is not distracted.

‘From Guilford, not Bromley. Father’s a history teacher. Mother’s a museum curator. His grandfather, though, was a titan of industry in the post-War years. Aeronautical engineering business in Bristol, bought by McDonnell Douglas, bought by Boeing. Normal stuff when descended from privilege — good school, Grade Eight viola,  county tennis, economics at SOAS, Masters at LSE in political economy. Very bog. Very standard. Should’ve ended up in marketing, diplomatic service, artisanal cheese, whatever.’

He flips a sheet, gulps at his scotch.

‘Not our Leo, a.k.a. Louis Geoffrey Rowland Prior. The dot-com bubble bursts at just the right time, or the wrong time you might say, right as he curls his outsized limbs under an academic desk, does his saintly undergrad dissertation on corporate governance, his virtuous postgrad on regulatory reform. And then 2007 rolls into 2008 and with it the banking crisis and the Great Recession, and where do we find young master Longshanks? In a tent in 2011 outside St Paul’s occupying London for five months behind a bloody Guy Fawkes mask, making daily payments into the Bank of Shitty Ideas. Then 2012, the camp is broken up and Louis le Prieur disappears, becomes a ghost, a myth.’

She grabs the document from him — he was not reading it anyway, has it memorised, it would seem — and she speeds down each page from line to line and the lower her finger falls the higher the horror rises, from stomach to chest to throat to teeth to temples until it reaches her scalp and yanks tight.

‘Not a year later, Leander Abbott so-called appears on the job market doing the rounds with a Persil-white resumé of premium quality and wearing a mask of another kind, neat and tidy and eager to please, all six foot colossal of him. Targeted — technology, pharma, genetics, all the advanced science start-ups. Lands a few, builds credibility into the legend, and in the fullness of time we find him at Secronia. The rest, you know.’

‘I can’t believe it,’ she says, still leafing through the dossier, backwards, forwards, back again.

‘All true.’

‘I can’t believe it. I’ve been so blind. How can I have …?’

‘It’s taken some bloody work to find this stuff, properly sanitised his old life.’

She tosses the papers at the coffee table where they collapse then tumble to the floor.

’I just can’t believe it,’ she says though she is in another place where rage and regret are wrestling in a cage.

Guy stoops to gather the sheets, stuffs them back into the wallet.

‘So, what now?’ he says.

‘Well …’

‘Coz I think we need to play it carefully.’

‘Yes, but Guy I…’

‘We’ve got to understand the damage before we do anything. So no confrontations yet, OK.’

‘Guy, there’s …’

‘No rash moves. Agreed? We need time to work out where we’re exposed, what’s at stake, plan our steps carefully.’

‘Guy. Stop.’

He stops, looks at her.

‘You’re shaking,’ he says, and she realises that she is, quite violently, balls her hands then clasps them to stop.

‘Let me get you that drink’

‘Guy, stop. Stop.’ She cannot look at him until she can. ‘There’s something …’

The doorbell chimes.

She turns, unsure whether she should hope. But the other racehorse, he has a key.

She stands, and she has to walk through the kitchen to reach the front door. Through the window she’s a car parked outside, white and blue and yellow. She hears Guy behind her.

Hannah pauses, to catch her breath or make time stop. There’s a fluttering in her abdomen, as if an echo from a future she cannot comprehend. She pulls open the door. Two police officers on the step, a woman and, behind, a younger man.

‘There’s been an accident. May we come in?’ the woman says.
